Small business cybersecurity checklist, mapped to CISA CPG 2.0

50 controls insurers and security guidance commonly ask about, in 8 groups. 15 of them cite a CISA Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goal by code and official title.

A readiness self-check, not an audit. This is a structured self-report against published guidance. It is not a security audit, a penetration test, a certification or a compliance determination, and nothing here promises a particular insurance outcome. Underwriting is each insurer's decision, made on that insurer's own application. For coverage questions talk to a licensed broker; for security work, to your IT or security lead.

Each control shows the question as an insurance application would ask it, what it means in plain terms, the CISA goal it maps to (where the app cites one) and a weight from 1 to 3. The weight is how much the app counts the control when it scores readiness: heavier controls move the score more.

Access Control

CPG 3.FMFA on email (weight 3 of 3)

The question: Is multi-factor authentication (MFA) enforced on every company email account?

Every login to company email requires a second factor (an app code, a hardware key, a push approval) in addition to a password, for every user, with no exceptions.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.F Implement Multifactor Authentication (MFA). CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: FBI IC3 Internet Crime Report, BEC section.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Compromised email is the single most common entry point insurers see for both business email compromise (wire-fraud) and ransomware claims - a stolen password alone should not be enough to get in.

  • Turn on MFA enforcement (not just "available") in your email admin console for every mailbox, including shared/service mailboxes.
  • Use an authenticator app or hardware key rather than SMS where the platform supports it.
  • Remove any legacy authentication protocols that let apps bypass MFA.

CPG 3.FMFA on remote access (weight 3 of 3)

The question: Is MFA required for all remote access into company systems (VPN, remote desktop tools, cloud admin consoles)?

Any way an employee (or an attacker) can reach internal systems from outside the office requires MFA, not just a username and password.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.F Implement Multifactor Authentication (MFA). CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.AA-03).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Remote access without MFA is one of the most frequently cited gaps in ransomware claim investigations - it is a direct path from a stolen password to full network access.

  • Enable MFA on the VPN or remote-access gateway itself, not just on the apps behind it.
  • Require MFA on cloud provider admin consoles (AWS/Azure/Google Workspace/Microsoft 365).
  • Disable direct internet-facing remote desktop where possible; require it to go through the MFA-protected gateway.

MFA on privileged accounts (weight 3 of 3)

The question: Do domain admin, IT admin, and other privileged accounts specifically require MFA?

Accounts with elevated permissions (able to change security settings, access all data, or manage other users) have MFA enforced, even if the organization has not rolled it out to every account yet.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 6 (Access Control Management).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A privileged account is the highest-value target for an attacker - compromising one can undo every other control on this list at once.

  • Identify every account with domain admin, global admin, or root-equivalent access.
  • Enforce MFA on those accounts first if MFA cannot yet be rolled out everywhere at once.
  • Consider a separate, MFA-only admin account distinct from that person’s day-to-day login.

No shared admin credentials (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Does every administrator use their own named account rather than a shared/generic admin login?

Nobody logs in as a generic "admin" account that multiple people know the password to; every admin action is traceable to one named person.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 5 (Account Management).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Shared credentials make it impossible to tell who did what during an incident, and they are far more likely to leak (written down, reused, left with a departed employee).

  • Create a separate admin account per person who needs elevated access.
  • Retire shared/generic admin logins; rotate their passwords once retired.
  • Log admin actions by account so activity is attributable.

CPG 3.BStrong, unique password policy (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is there an enforced password policy requiring long, unique passwords (and is password reuse across accounts prevented)?

Passwords are required to meet a real minimum length, are not reused across accounts, and service/machine accounts have their own unique passwords distinct from any person’s login.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.B Establish Minimum Password Strength. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 5.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Weak or reused passwords are a routine way a single leaked credential (from an unrelated breach elsewhere) turns into access to your systems, independent of whether MFA is in place everywhere yet.

  • Enforce a system-level minimum password length (CISA recommends 15+ characters) rather than relying on complexity rules alone.
  • Block reuse of the same password across accounts, and give service/machine accounts their own unique credentials.
  • Consider a company password manager so long, unique passwords are actually practical for staff to use.

Least privilege + prompt offboarding (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is access limited to what each role actually needs, and revoked promptly when someone leaves or changes roles?

People have access to only the systems and data their job requires, and that access is removed on their last day, not weeks later.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.AA-05); CIS Controls v8, Control 5.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Over-provisioned or stale access widens the blast radius of any single compromised account and is a common finding after an insider or departed-employee incident.

  • Periodically review who has access to what and remove anything no longer needed.
  • Make access revocation the last step of the employee offboarding checklist (see also the offboarding-process control), completed the same day.
  • Avoid granting broad "just in case" access.

Default vendor passwords changed (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Have the default/vendor-supplied passwords been changed on all devices, accounts, and software (routers, firewalls, cameras, admin consoles)?

Nothing on the network is still running with the password it shipped with from the manufacturer; every built-in or default account has had its password changed to a strong, unique one (or been disabled).

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 4 (Secure Configuration) and Control 5 (Account Management).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Default credentials are published, widely known, and among the first things automated attacks try - a device still using its factory password is effectively unlocked from the internet.

  • Inventory network gear and admin consoles (routers, firewalls, NAS, cameras, printers, cloud/appliance admin logins) and change every default password to a strong, unique one.
  • Disable or rename built-in default accounts that are not needed.
  • Make "change the default password" a required step whenever new equipment or software is set up.

Lockout / detection on failed logins (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Do accounts lock (or alert) after repeated failed login attempts, to slow down password-guessing attacks?

After a number of wrong password attempts an account is temporarily locked or the attempt is flagged, so an attacker cannot simply try thousands of passwords against a login unnoticed.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 6 (Access Control Management) and Control 8 (Audit Log Management).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Automated brute-force and password-spraying attacks rely on being able to guess repeatedly and silently; a lockout or alert threshold turns that from an easy win into a noisy, detectable one.

  • Enable an account-lockout or throttling threshold in your directory/identity provider and on internet-facing logins.
  • Alert on bursts of failed logins or on lockouts affecting many accounts at once.
  • Balance the threshold so it stops attacks without constantly locking out legitimate staff.

Automatic screen lock (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Do workstations and laptops automatically lock the screen after a short period of inactivity, requiring the password to return?

Devices lock themselves after a few idle minutes so an unattended, unlocked machine - in an office, a coffee shop, a home - is not left open to anyone who walks up to it.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 4 (Secure Configuration of Enterprise Assets and Software).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. An unlocked, unattended device bypasses every login control you have; automatic session locking is a cheap, standard baseline that closes that everyday gap.

  • Set an automatic screen-lock timeout (for example, 15 minutes or less) fleet-wide through your device-management tool or OS policy.
  • Require a password, PIN, or biometric to unlock, not just a swipe.
  • Remind staff to lock their screen manually when they step away.

Standard users are not local admins (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Do everyday user accounts run without local administrator rights on their own machines (admin rights granted only when specifically needed)?

People do their normal work in a standard (non-admin) account; installing software or changing system settings requires a separate elevation step, rather than every user being a full administrator of their own computer all day.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 5 (Account Management); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.AA-05).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. When a standard user is also a local admin, malware that lands in their session inherits full control of the machine - removing standing admin rights sharply limits what a single compromised login can do.

  • Convert everyday accounts to standard users and remove local administrator membership.
  • Provide a controlled way to elevate for specific tasks (a separate admin credential or a just-in-time elevation tool) rather than blanket admin rights.
  • Review who still has local admin and remove it where it is not genuinely required.

Endpoint Security

CPG 4.AEDR on endpoints and servers (weight 3 of 3)

The question: Is Endpoint Detection & Response (EDR) or an equivalent modern tool deployed on all workstations and servers?

A monitoring agent runs on every computer and server that can detect and often automatically stop malicious activity, not just signature-based antivirus.

CISA CPG 2.0: 4.A Establish Malicious Code Detection. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 10.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. EDR is now one of the most commonly required controls for binding a cyber policy at all - it is what typically catches ransomware in progress rather than after the fact.

  • Deploy an EDR product to every workstation and server, including remote/BYOD devices that touch company data.
  • Confirm the agent is actually reporting in (not just installed) across the whole fleet.
  • Route EDR alerts somewhere they get reviewed, not just logged.

CPG 2.BDocumented patch cadence (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is there a defined, followed timeline for applying security patches (especially critical/high-severity ones)?

The organization has an actual target - e.g. critical patches within a set number of days - and can show patches going out on that schedule, not an ad-hoc "whenever."

CISA CPG 2.0: 2.B Mitigate Known Vulnerabilities. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 7.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Unpatched known vulnerabilities are one of the most common ways ransomware groups get initial access; insurers want evidence of a real cadence, not just intent.

  • Set a written target (e.g. critical patches within 72 hours, others within 30 days).
  • Turn on automatic updates where feasible for OS and common software.
  • Track patch status across the fleet so gaps are visible, not assumed away.

Full-disk encryption (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Are laptops and mobile devices that hold company data encrypted at the disk level (e.g. BitLocker, FileVault)?

If a laptop is lost or stolen, its data cannot be read without the device password/key, because the whole disk is encrypted.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.DS-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Device loss/theft is a common, low-sophistication cause of a data-breach claim; disk encryption often turns a lost laptop from a reportable breach into a non-event.

  • Turn on BitLocker (Windows) or FileVault (Mac) fleet-wide via your device management tool.
  • Confirm mobile devices (phones/tablets) with company email or data are encrypted by default (most modern OSes do this automatically once a passcode is set).
  • Store recovery keys centrally, not on the device itself.

CPG 2.ANo end-of-life systems (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Are there any operating systems or critical software still in production past their vendor end-of-life/support date?

Every OS and critical application in use is still receiving security patches from its vendor; nothing is running on a version the vendor has stopped supporting.

CISA CPG 2.0: 2.A Manage Organizational Assets. CISA's CPG page

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. An unsupported system can never be patched against newly discovered vulnerabilities, which makes it a standing, unfixable weak point.

  • Inventory every OS and critical software version in production.
  • Flag anything approaching or past its vendor end-of-life date.
  • Plan and budget upgrades before support actually ends, not after.

Hardware & software inventory (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is there a maintained inventory of the devices (computers, servers, network gear) and software actually in use across the business?

The organization keeps an up-to-date list of the hardware connected to its network and the software installed on it - so it knows what it is actually responsible for protecting, patching, and watching.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 1 (Inventory and Control of Enterprise Assets) and Control 2 (Inventory and Control of Software Assets); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Identify (ID.AM-01, ID.AM-02).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. You cannot protect, patch, or detect a compromise on a device or application you do not know exists; an accurate inventory is the foundation nearly every other control depends on.

  • Build a list of enterprise assets (workstations, servers, network devices, and the software on them), ideally pulled automatically from your device-management or discovery tooling rather than kept by hand.
  • Flag anything unknown or unauthorized that appears on the network for review.
  • Update the inventory as devices and software are added or retired, not once a year.

Secure baseline configuration (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Are devices and software set up from a secure baseline - unnecessary services, ports, and features turned off - rather than left at default settings?

Machines and applications are configured to a hardened standard (a documented baseline), with default sample accounts, unused services, and unnecessary open ports disabled, rather than run however they came out of the box.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 4 (Secure Configuration of Enterprise Assets and Software); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.PS-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Default configurations are built for easy setup, not security; leftover services and open ports are exactly the loose ends attackers probe for, and a consistent baseline removes them everywhere at once.

  • Adopt a secure configuration baseline (for example a CIS Benchmark) for your common operating systems and key applications.
  • Disable services, ports, protocols, and default accounts that are not needed.
  • Apply the baseline through device management so new machines start hardened, and check for drift over time.

Application control / allowlisting (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is there control over which software is allowed to run on company machines, rather than any program being free to execute?

The organization limits what can run - either by explicitly allowing only approved applications, or at minimum by controlling and reviewing what gets installed - so unknown or unauthorized programs do not simply execute.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 2 (Inventory and Control of Software Assets); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.PS-05).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Ransomware and other malware are, ultimately, unauthorized programs running; controlling what is allowed to execute stops a whole class of attacks before an endpoint tool even has to detect them.

  • Where feasible, enable application allowlisting so only approved software can run on sensitive machines (servers, finance workstations).
  • At a minimum, restrict who can install software and maintain an approved-software list tied to your software inventory.
  • Verify the source and integrity of new software before it is approved and deployed.

Managed mobile / BYOD devices (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Are phones, tablets, and laptops that access company data managed (enrolled in an MDM/device-management tool with security policies enforced)?

Devices that reach company email or data - including personal phones used for work - are enrolled in a management tool that can enforce a passcode, encryption, and, if the device is lost, remotely wipe company data.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 4 (Secure Configuration of Enterprise Assets and Software); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.PS-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Lost or stolen mobile devices and unmanaged personal devices are a common, low-sophistication route to company data; centralized management lets the business enforce baseline protections and cut off a device that goes missing.

  • Enroll company (and work-used personal) devices in a mobile-device-management or endpoint-management tool.
  • Enforce passcode, encryption, and automatic-lock policies, and require the device to be up to date to access company data.
  • Enable remote lock/wipe of company data for lost or stolen devices and for offboarding.

Backup & Recovery

CPG 3.OOffline/immutable backups (weight 3 of 3)

The question: Do backups include a copy that is offline, air-gapped, or immutable, so ransomware on the network cannot reach and encrypt it too?

At least one backup copy cannot be modified or deleted by anything running on the regular network - either because it is physically disconnected or because it is stored in a write-once/immutable mode.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.O Maintain System Backups & Restoration Ability. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 11.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Modern ransomware actively hunts for and encrypts or deletes connected backups first; a backup an attacker can also destroy is not a real recovery path, and this is one of the single highest-weighted controls insurers screen for.

  • Add an offline (tape, disconnected drive) or cloud-immutable (object-lock/WORM) copy to your backup rotation.
  • Make sure backup admin credentials are separate from everyday domain admin credentials, so a compromised admin account can’t also delete backups.
  • Confirm the immutable/offline copy is actually current, not stale.

Backups tested by restoring (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Are backups periodically tested by actually restoring data, not just confirmed to have run?

On a defined schedule, someone actually restores a real file, system, or environment from backup and confirms it works - a backup job "completing successfully" every night is not the same thing.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Recover (RC.RP-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A large share of failed disaster recoveries are discovered only during a real incident, when it is too late - insurers want proof recovery actually works.

  • Schedule a periodic (e.g. quarterly) test restore of a sample of backed-up data or a full critical system.
  • Document the result - what was restored, how long it took, whether it worked.
  • Fix anything the test reveals before the next real incident does.

Recovery objectives documented (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Are recovery time objective (RTO) and recovery point objective (RPO) documented for critical systems?

For each critical system, there is a written answer to "how long can this be down" (RTO) and "how much data can we afford to lose" (RPO) - the numbers that actually drive backup frequency and recovery priority.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Recover (RC.RP).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Without documented objectives, "we have backups" doesn’t tell an insurer whether recovery would actually meet the business’s real tolerance for downtime or data loss.

  • List the systems that would hurt the business most if they went down.
  • For each, write down an acceptable downtime and acceptable data-loss window.
  • Check that current backup frequency and recovery plans can actually meet those numbers.

Backups encrypted (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Are backups encrypted, both in transit to the backup destination and at rest once stored?

A stolen or accessed backup copy is unreadable without the encryption key - encryption applies both while the backup is being transferred and while it sits in storage.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.DS-01/02).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Backups often contain a full copy of sensitive data; an unencrypted backup that leaks is functionally the same size breach as the live system.

  • Confirm your backup tool encrypts data in transit (TLS) and at rest by default, and that encryption is actually turned on.
  • Store encryption keys separately from the backup data itself.

Email & Phishing Defense

CPG 3.LAdvanced email/phishing filtering (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is there email filtering beyond a basic spam filter - one that specifically targets phishing, malicious attachments, and links?

Inbound email is screened by a tool built to catch phishing and malware specifically (link rewriting/sandboxing, attachment detonation), not just bulk-spam filtering.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.L Enable Email Security. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: FBI IC3 Internet Crime Report, phishing statistics.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Phishing remains the top reported entry vector in FBI IC3’s annual data; a purpose-built filter meaningfully reduces how many malicious emails ever reach an employee.

  • Enable your email platform’s advanced threat/phishing protection tier if you are only on the basic tier.
  • Consider a dedicated email security add-on if your platform’s built-in filtering is limited.
  • Review what is getting through periodically, not just what gets blocked.

CPG 3.LSPF / DKIM / DMARC configured (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Are SPF, DKIM, and DMARC configured for every company email-sending domain?

These are DNS records that let receiving mail servers verify an email claiming to be from your domain is actually authorized, and tell them what to do if it isn’t.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.L Enable Email Security. CISA's CPG page

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Without DMARC, attackers can spoof your own domain to phish your customers or partners - a reputational and liability exposure, not just an internal one.

  • Publish SPF and DKIM records for every domain that sends mail.
  • Publish a DMARC record, starting at a monitoring policy (`p=none`) and tightening to quarantine/reject once you’ve confirmed legitimate mail passes.
  • Review DMARC aggregate reports periodically to catch unauthorized senders.

CPG 3.JSecurity awareness training (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Do all employees receive regular security-awareness training (at least annually)?

Every employee, not just IT staff, gets periodic training on recognizing phishing, social engineering, and safe handling of company data and credentials.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.J Implement Cybersecurity Training. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.AT).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Human error is a leading factor in successful attacks; trained staff are measurably less likely to click a phishing link or fall for a wire-transfer scam.

  • Enroll all staff in a security-awareness training program at hire and at least annually after.
  • Cover phishing, password hygiene, and reporting suspicious activity specifically.
  • Track completion so gaps are visible.

CPG 3.JPhishing simulations (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Are simulated phishing exercises run periodically to test and reinforce staff awareness?

The organization sends its own staff realistic-but-fake phishing emails on a regular cadence and tracks who clicks, then follows up with targeted training.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.J Implement Cybersecurity Training. CISA's CPG page

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Simulations are the way to verify training is actually working, rather than just having happened once.

  • Run a phishing simulation campaign at least a few times a year using a reputable platform.
  • Give immediate, non-punitive feedback to anyone who clicks, with a short refresher.
  • Track click/report rates over time to show improvement.

Web / DNS content filtering (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is web traffic filtered (for example DNS filtering) to block known-malicious and risky websites before a browser can reach them?

When someone clicks a bad link or a piece of malware tries to phone home, a filtering layer blocks the connection to known-malicious or high-risk domains, rather than letting the browser or program connect freely.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 9 (Email and Web Browser Protections); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.IR-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Many attacks depend on a browser reaching a malicious site or malware reaching its command-and-control server; blocking those connections stops phishing-link and malware payloads even when a user has already clicked.

  • Turn on a DNS-filtering or web-security service that blocks known-malicious and newly registered domains for all company devices, including remote ones.
  • Block obviously high-risk categories that have no business use.
  • Review what is being blocked periodically so filtering is tuned, not just turned on.

Network Security

Perimeter firewall, default-deny (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is a firewall in place at the network perimeter with a default-deny posture (only explicitly allowed traffic gets through)?

Traffic into the network is blocked by default; only specific, intentional exceptions are allowed through, rather than everything being open unless specifically blocked.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 12 (Network Infrastructure Management).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A default-allow network is directly exposed to internet scanning and opportunistic attacks the moment any service is misconfigured.

  • Confirm the perimeter firewall’s default rule is deny/block, with an explicit allow-list of needed ports/services.
  • Periodically review firewall rules for ones that are no longer needed.

CPG 3.SRDP not exposed to the internet (weight 3 of 3)

The question: Is Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) kept off the open internet - only reachable through a VPN, MFA-protected gateway, or jump host?

Nobody can reach a company machine’s remote-desktop port directly from the public internet; it is only reachable after first connecting through a protected gateway.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.S Secure Internet-Facing Devices. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: FBI/CISA joint ransomware advisories.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Directly internet-exposed RDP is one of the single most common ransomware entry points found in claims - it is actively and automatically scanned for by attackers.

  • Scan your own public IP ranges for open RDP (port 3389) and close any found.
  • Put RDP access behind the company VPN or a dedicated MFA-protected remote-access gateway instead.
  • Disable RDP entirely on machines that don’t need it.

Network segmentation (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is the network segmented so that, e.g., guest/IoT devices and core business systems are not on the same flat network?

Different classes of device or sensitivity live on separate network segments, so a compromise on one segment doesn’t automatically reach every other system.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 12.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A flat network lets an attacker who lands on one low-value device (a guest laptop, a smart TV) move laterally straight to critical systems.

  • Put guest Wi-Fi and IoT devices on a separate VLAN from business systems.
  • Separate more sensitive systems (finance, production servers) onto their own segment where feasible.
  • Restrict what can talk between segments to only what is actually needed.

CPG 2.BVulnerability scanning (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Are internet-facing systems regularly scanned for known vulnerabilities?

Anything reachable from the public internet (a website, a VPN gateway, a mail server) is periodically scanned by an automated tool that checks for known, unpatched vulnerabilities.

CISA CPG 2.0: 2.B Mitigate Known Vulnerabilities. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 7.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Scanning is how you find the same weaknesses an attacker’s automated tools would find, before they do.

  • Run a vulnerability scan against all internet-facing assets on a regular schedule (e.g. monthly).
  • Track and remediate findings, prioritizing anything rated critical/high.
  • Re-scan after major changes to confirm fixes actually took effect.

Public-facing web application protection (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Are public-facing web applications that handle sensitive data protected against common web attacks (e.g. a web application firewall, or a secure development/testing process)?

Any website or web app the business runs itself (not a third-party SaaS tool) that takes customer data or logins has protection against common attack patterns like SQL injection and cross-site scripting - either a web application firewall in front of it, or a real secure-development and testing process behind it.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 16 (Application Software Security).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A vulnerable public-facing application is a direct, internet-reachable path to sensitive data that bypasses network perimeter controls entirely.

  • If you run your own customer-facing web app or portal, put a web application firewall in front of it if you don’t already have one.
  • Include basic security testing (even an automated scanner) in how the app is built or updated, not just after an incident.
  • If the business only uses third-party SaaS tools and runs no web application of its own, this control has no gap to close - note that in the app.

Network monitoring / intrusion detection (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is network traffic monitored for intrusions and unusual activity (an IDS/IPS or equivalent network monitoring), not just filtered by a firewall?

Beyond blocking traffic at the firewall, something is actively watching network activity for the signs of an intrusion - unexpected connections, lateral movement, data leaving in bulk - and can alert on or block it.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 13 (Network Monitoring and Defense); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Detect (DE.CM-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Firewalls decide what is allowed in; monitoring is how you notice an attacker who is already inside. Faster detection is one of the largest factors in how much a breach ultimately costs.

  • Deploy network intrusion detection/prevention or a network-monitoring capability covering key network chokepoints.
  • Send its alerts to the same place your other security logs are reviewed (see the centralized logging control) so they are actually seen.
  • Tune it to reduce noise so real alerts are not lost in false positives.

Periodic penetration testing (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is a penetration test performed periodically to actually attempt to find and exploit weaknesses, beyond automated vulnerability scanning?

On a defined cadence, a skilled tester (internal or hired) actively tries to break in the way an attacker would - chaining together weaknesses to see how far they can get - rather than only running an automated scan for known issues.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 18 (Penetration Testing); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Identify (ID.RA-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Scanning finds known, individual vulnerabilities; a penetration test shows whether they can actually be exploited together to reach sensitive systems, which is a more realistic measure of exposure.

  • Arrange a penetration test of internet-facing systems and critical internal systems on a periodic basis (annually is common), scoped to your real risk.
  • Prioritize and remediate the findings, and re-test the important ones to confirm they are fixed.
  • Keep the report - insurers and some contracts ask to see that testing happens.

Secure wireless networks (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is company Wi-Fi secured with modern encryption (WPA2/WPA3), with guest Wi-Fi kept separate from the business network?

Wireless networks use current encryption and strong keys (not open or outdated WEP/WPA), and visitors connect to a separate guest network that cannot reach internal business systems.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 12 (Network Infrastructure Management); NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.IR-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A weakly secured or flat wireless network gives anyone within range - or any guest device - a foothold onto the business network without ever touching the firewall from the internet.

  • Confirm all company access points use WPA2 or WPA3 with a strong passphrase or enterprise authentication, and retire any open or WEP/legacy-WPA networks.
  • Run guest/visitor Wi-Fi on its own isolated network that cannot reach internal systems (see the network-segmentation control).
  • Watch for and remove rogue or unauthorized access points.

Incident Response

CPG 1.CWritten incident response plan (weight 3 of 3)

The question: Does the organization have a written incident response (IR) plan?

There is an actual document describing what to do when a security incident is suspected or confirmed - not an assumption that people will figure it out in the moment.

CISA CPG 2.0: 1.C Maintain Incident Response Plans. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Respond (RS.MA).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Response speed and quality in the first hours of an incident materially affects both damage and claim cost; insurers want to see a plan exists before it’s needed.

  • Write a plan covering: how to detect/report a suspected incident, who is on the response team, immediate containment steps, and communication steps.
  • Keep a copy accessible even if normal systems are down (e.g. printed, or on a device outside the network).
  • Review and update it at least annually.

IR plan tested/exercised (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Has the incident response plan been tested or run through a tabletop exercise in the last 12 months?

The team has actually walked through a realistic scenario (e.g. "we just got a ransomware note") using the written plan, not just filed the document away.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Respond (RS.MA-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. An untested plan routinely turns out to have gaps (missing contacts, unclear authority to pay/not pay, no one who knows the actual backup restore steps) that only surface during a real incident otherwise.

  • Schedule a tabletop exercise at least once a year with the actual people who’d be involved.
  • Pick a realistic scenario (ransomware, BEC, lost laptop) and walk through the plan step by step.
  • Update the plan with whatever the exercise reveals is missing or unclear.

Breach contacts named in advance (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Does the IR plan name who to call - forensics firm, breach counsel, PR, the cyber insurer/broker - before an incident happens?

The plan lists actual names, firms, and phone numbers to call in the first hours, decided calmly in advance rather than looked up under pressure.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Respond (RS.CO).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Many policies require using a pre-approved panel of forensics/legal vendors, and delay in engaging them can affect both the response and coverage.

  • Confirm what forensics/legal/PR vendors, if any, your cyber policy requires or recommends, and add them to the plan by name.
  • Include your broker/insurer’s claims-reporting phone number directly in the plan.
  • Make sure the plan is reachable even if email/phones are affected.

Insurer notified early, not after the fact (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Does the plan require notifying the cyber insurer/broker at the start of a suspected incident, not after it’s resolved?

The very first steps of the plan include contacting the insurer’s claims line - many policies require notice before incurring response costs for those costs to be covered.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in General cyber-policy claims-reporting practice - confirm your specific policy’s notice clause with your broker.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Late notification is one of the most common reasons a claim gets disputed; insurers want proof this is baked into the plan’s first steps, not an afterthought.

  • Add the insurer/broker’s claims-reporting number as one of the first steps in the IR plan, alongside internal escalation.
  • Confirm with your broker what your specific policy’s notice requirements and timeline are.

Vendor & Data Management

Sensitive data inventory (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is there an inventory of where sensitive data (customer PII, payment data, health data, etc.) actually lives?

The organization can say, concretely, which systems and vendors hold sensitive data - not just "somewhere in our cloud."

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Identify (ID.AM-07).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. You cannot scope a breach, notify the right people, or protect the right systems if you don’t know where the sensitive data is in the first place.

  • List every system, database, and vendor that stores or processes sensitive data.
  • Note what type of data each holds and roughly how much.
  • Revisit the inventory when new systems or vendors are added.

Encryption in transit (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is sensitive data encrypted in transit (e.g. TLS on every web/app connection that carries it)?

Data moving between systems, or between a user and a system, is encrypted the whole way, so it can’t be read if intercepted.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.DS-02).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Unencrypted transit is a straightforward, well-understood way for sensitive data to be intercepted, and a routine finding insurers screen for.

  • Confirm every customer-facing site/app enforces HTTPS (TLS), with no fallback to plain HTTP.
  • Confirm internal system-to-system connections carrying sensitive data are also encrypted.

Encryption at rest (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is sensitive data encrypted at rest in the databases/storage where it lives?

Sensitive data sitting in a database or file store is encrypted, so a stolen disk or an unauthorized direct data-store access doesn’t expose it in plain form.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.DS-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Encryption at rest is a standard baseline expectation and can also reduce breach-notification obligations in several state data-breach laws when data is unreadable.

  • Turn on encryption-at-rest in your database/cloud storage provider (most offer it natively).
  • Confirm backups of that data are encrypted too (see backup-encryption control).

Critical vendor security review (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Are critical vendors/subprocessors (especially ones with access to your data or systems) reviewed for their own security posture?

Before and periodically after onboarding a vendor that touches company data or systems, someone actually checks their security practices - not just their price and features.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Govern (GV.SC).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A large share of breaches originate through a third-party vendor rather than a direct attack - your exposure includes theirs.

  • Identify vendors with access to sensitive data or company systems.
  • Ask for evidence of their security practices (a SOC 2 report, security questionnaire, or similar) before onboarding.
  • Revisit critical vendor relationships periodically, not just once at signup.

Data retention & secure disposal (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is sensitive data kept only as long as it is actually needed and then securely disposed of, rather than retained indefinitely?

The business holds sensitive data only while there is a real reason to, and when data or a device is no longer needed it is securely destroyed (wiped, shredded) - old records and decommissioned drives are not just left sitting around.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 3 (Data Protection).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Every record you still hold is a record that can be stolen; data you no longer keep cannot be part of a breach, so minimizing and disposing of stale data directly shrinks the size of any incident.

  • Set retention periods for categories of sensitive data and delete or archive data once its period ends.
  • Securely wipe or destroy drives, backups, and paper records when they are retired, rather than discarding them intact.
  • Tie this to the data inventory so you know what exists and where before deciding what to keep.

Vendor breach-notification terms (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Do contracts with vendors that hold your data require them to notify you promptly if they suffer a breach affecting it?

Agreements with vendors and subprocessors that store or process company/customer data include a clause obligating them to tell you, within a set time, if their systems are breached in a way that touches your data.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Govern (GV.SC); CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 15 (Service Provider Management).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A large share of breaches reach a business through a vendor; if the vendor is not contractually bound to tell you quickly, you can miss legal notification deadlines and discover the incident far too late.

  • Check that data-handling vendor contracts include a breach-notification clause with a defined timeframe.
  • Add or negotiate the clause for critical vendors that lack one, especially any holding customer PII or payment/health data.
  • Keep a current contact at each critical vendor so a notification actually reaches the right person.

Governance & Training

Written information security policy (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Does the organization have a written information security policy distributed to staff?

There is an actual document setting expectations - acceptable use, password requirements, data handling, reporting obligations - that employees have received and acknowledged.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Govern (GV.PO).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A written policy is the baseline evidence that security expectations are a real organizational standard, not an informal habit that varies by team.

  • Write (or adopt and adapt a template for) a company information security policy.
  • Distribute it to all staff and have them acknowledge receipt.
  • Review and update it at least annually.

CPG 3.QCentralized logging & monitoring (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Are logs from critical systems centrally collected and actively monitored (a SIEM or equivalent), not just stored locally?

Security-relevant logs (logins, admin actions, alerts) from important systems flow into one place someone actually watches or that generates alerts, rather than sitting unreviewed on each individual machine.

CISA CPG 2.0: 3.Q Maintain Log Collection & Storage. CISA's CPG page

Also referenced: CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 8.

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Detection speed is one of the biggest cost drivers in a breach - the longer an intrusion goes unnoticed, the more it typically costs to remediate.

  • Route logs from critical systems (domain controllers, firewalls, EDR, cloud admin activity) to a central log tool or SIEM.
  • Set up alerting for high-risk events (e.g. new admin account created, MFA disabled, mass file access).
  • Assign someone to actually review alerts, not just collect logs.

Documented offboarding process (weight 2 of 3)

The question: Is there a documented process that revokes a departing employee’s access the same day they leave?

Offboarding is a checklist, not a memory - it lists every system to disable access to, and it happens on the employee’s last day, every time.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in CIS Critical Security Controls v8, Control 5; NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Protect (PR.AA-05).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. Former-employee accounts left active are a recurring, entirely preventable cause of unauthorized access incidents.

  • Write an offboarding checklist covering every system, app, and physical access the person had.
  • Assign ownership so it actually runs on the last day, not "sometime that week."
  • Periodically audit active accounts against current staff to catch anything missed.

Named owner for security (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Is there a specific person (or role) formally responsible and accountable for cybersecurity, rather than it being nobody’s clear job?

Someone is named as responsible for security decisions and for making sure controls actually get done - it is a defined role, even if part-time or outsourced, not an assumption that "IT" or "the owner" handles it informally.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Govern (GV.RR-02).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. When no one owns security, controls quietly lapse and incidents have no clear decision-maker; a named owner is the baseline evidence that security is actively managed rather than left to chance.

  • Assign cybersecurity responsibility to a specific person or role and communicate it, even in a small business (it can be a part-time or fractional/vCISO role).
  • Make sure that person has the authority and time to actually run the controls, not just the title.
  • Have them own this readiness review and the plan to close its gaps.

Periodic risk assessment (weight 1 of 3)

The question: Does the business periodically assess its cybersecurity risks (what could go wrong, how likely, how bad) rather than only reacting after something happens?

On a recurring basis, the organization steps back and identifies its biggest cyber risks - which systems and data matter most, what threats they face, where the weaknesses are - and uses that to decide where to focus effort and spend.

CISA CPG 2.0: the app cites no CPG goal for this control. It is grounded in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, Identify (ID.RA-01).

Why insurers ask, and steps to close the gap

Why it is asked. A risk assessment is how a business prioritizes limited resources on the controls that matter most for its situation; its absence usually means security is ad hoc and driven by whatever went wrong last.

  • Run a lightweight risk assessment at least annually: list critical systems/data, the main threats to them, and the current gaps.
  • Use the results to prioritize which controls to close first (this readiness score is a useful input).
  • Record it so you can show risk decisions were deliberate, and revisit after major business or system changes.
