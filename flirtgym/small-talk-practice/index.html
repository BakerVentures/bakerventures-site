The six skills, and why these six

Small talk is usually treated as one undifferentiated thing you are either good at or not. It is six separate habits, every one of them trainable on its own, and most people are missing one or two rather than all six.

DrillWhat it trainsWhat it fixes
Statement, not questionReplying with something of your own instead of another questionThe interview. Two people asking questions is not a conversation, and the one asking them all is doing all the work
Pick the threadHearing which single word in a sentence is worth pulling onResponding to the whole sentence, which produces a general answer and then silence
Say something trueOffering one unhedged fact about yourself at normal speedBeing completely unknowable, which reads as either boring or guarded and ends things either way
Tease with an exitLight disagreement that leaves her an easy way outBoth failure modes at once: agreeing with everything, and teasing with no exit, which is just an insult
Guess instead of askingMaking a specific assumption out loud and being happy to be wrongQuestion fatigue. A guess gives her something to correct, which is much easier than answering
Land the planTurning something she said into a concrete thing with a day attachedThe good conversation that goes nowhere, because nobody said anything specific out loud

How to actually run this

Ten minutes, out loud, somewhere you are alone. Do not stop to think of a better answer. A mediocre answer delivered at speed is a successful rep; a perfect answer thirty seconds late is a failed one, because thirty seconds late is the thing you are trying to fix.

  • Say it at normal volume. Under your breath trains a different muscle. The voice is part of it.
  • Do not skip the ones you find uncomfortable. The drill you want to skip is the skill you are missing.
  • Stop at ten minutes. This gets worse with volume, not better. Daily and short beats an hour once.
  • Repeat the same prompt twice. The second answer is reliably better than the first, and noticing why is most of the value.

What a drill cannot do

Nobody is reacting. No answer here lands badly, goes quiet, or surprises you, and those are the three things that actually happen. So this is a warm-up: it makes the mechanics automatic so that the live attention goes on the person instead of on what to say next. It does not make the first real attempt feel easy.

If freezing is the specific problem rather than running out of things to say, this page is about that. If it is first-date territory, the question ladder is the companion piece to these drills. And if the thing you are rehearsing is a first message rather than a conversation, the opener score grades those.

Questions

Do I have to say it out loud?

Yes, and it is the whole point. Thinking of a good reply is not the skill that fails you in the room. Producing one at conversational speed, with your own voice making the sound, is the skill that fails, and it is a different one. Saying these in your head trains the part that already works.

Why twenty seconds?

Because a real pause before a reply is about two seconds, and twenty is long enough to get something out without being long enough to draft it. The timer is not a test you pass. It is there to stop you writing an essay in your head, which is exactly the habit that produces the long silence you are trying to fix.

Is anything recorded or stored?

No. There is no microphone, no recording, no account and no storage. The drills are in the page and the counter is held in memory only, so reloading the page starts you at zero. Nothing you say or do here leaves the device, and none of it reaches us.

How much of this actually transfers to a real conversation?

The mechanics transfer and the nerves do not. What a drill can build is the habit of replying with a statement instead of a question, hearing which word in a sentence is worth pulling on, and having a plan ready in a form that is easy to decline. What it cannot do is put another person in front of you, which is why this is a warm-up rather than a substitute.
