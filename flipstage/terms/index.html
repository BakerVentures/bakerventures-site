What FlipStage is — a surface, not a presentation

FlipStage splits your phone's screen into two facing halves. The half pointing away from you shows the current slide, rotated so it reads upright from the other side of a table. The half pointing at you shows your speaker notes, an elapsed timer and the controls. You build a deck from photos in your library, or start from the sample deck, and move through it with Prev and Next. That is the entire product.

It is a display tool for presenting face to face, and nothing more. As a term of this agreement:

  • Keeping your notes private is a matter of where you point the phone. This is the one thing worth reading twice. FlipStage puts your notes on a screen that is, by design, lying on a table in front of another person. The app controls which half of the display shows what; it cannot control viewing angles, reflections, someone leaning over, or a camera in the room. If a note would damage you if the other person read it, don't type it into a device you are placing in front of them.

  • The timer is a convenience, not an instrument. It counts elapsed time and turns red once you pass a target you set. It is not calibrated, it is not a stopwatch of record, and it should not be relied on where a timing is scored, judged, or disputed — a moot, a pitch competition, a timed exam.
  • It does not record anything. FlipStage does not film, photograph, record audio, transcribe, or scroll text automatically. It shows a slide on one side and your notes on the other. Nothing about a conversation held over it is captured by the app.
  • The two-sided view needs the phone upright. It opens only when the window is taller than it is wide. In landscape you will not get the two facing halves, and that is how the app is built rather than a fault.
